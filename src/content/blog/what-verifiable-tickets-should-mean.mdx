"Verifiable" is doing a lot of work in ticketing marketing, and most of the
time it is not being made to mean anything specific. A barcode is verifiable. A
screenshot of a barcode is verifiable. Both check out and neither means
anything.

Here is the standard we are trying to hold.

## A verification that cannot be faked is one that checks the ledger

Every ticket Veritix issues corresponds to a record that is publicly checkable
on the Stellar network. Presenting at the door is not "does this string look
like a ticket" — it is "does this ticket exist, has it already been used, and is
the person presenting it the holder recorded against it".

That means a screenshot of a QR code fails. The code in the screenshot encodes a
claim, not a record. The person at the door is looking at the record on the
network, and there is no such thing as an image of one.

## The property that actually matters: single use

A forgeable ticket is one of two bad outcomes, and people only discuss the first.

The first is a ticket that does not exist — invented, or copied from a real
event and reprinted. The second is a real ticket used twice: a screenshot shared
with a friend, or a resale that never got revoked.

The second is the more common failure in practice, and it is the one a scanner
solves. A ticket is marked used in the same check that admits it, so the second
presentation of the same ticket is a failure, and the failure is recorded. Not
"should fail if nobody noticed first" — there is no window in which two doors
accept the same ticket.

## What verification does not give you

Being honest about the limits, because a security claim that overstates itself is
worse than no security claim.

- **It does not stop the original buyer transferring the ticket to someone
  else.** It stops them from *copying* it. If transfer is enabled for an event,
  the transfer is recorded and the new holder is who the ledger names. If it is
  not enabled, the ticket is not transferable and a transfer is a breach of the
  buyer's agreement with the organizer.
- **It does not stop resale.** A ticket resold through any channel is still a
  valid ticket. Resale is a pricing problem, not a forgery problem, and
  pretending a blockchain solves it would be a category error.
- **It does not tell the person at the door why a ticket failed to a
  useful degree.** "Already used at 19:42" is a much more useful message than
  "invalid", and it is the kind of detail that turns an argument into a
  conversation. It is worth doing properly and it is its own work.
- **It does not protect the buyer from an organizer who does not honour a
  ticket.** That is a contract problem, covered by the refund commitment published
  with the event and by the Terms — not something a ledger can fix.

## Why the boring version matters

There is a temptation, in a product like this, to describe verification in terms
that sound like more than they are. "Cryptographically provable ownership" is
technically true and practically useless to somebody working a door on a
Saturday night.

The version that holds up is the boring one: a ticket is a record, the record is
checked against the ledger, the record is marked used, and a second attempt
fails. If that is not impressive enough, the answer is not a better adjective.
It is a better door-side experience — clearer failure messages, an offline
fallback for a venue with no signal — which is where the actual work is.
