Every ticketing platform eventually has to explain where the money goes. Most of
them say "payouts processed" or "funds held securely", which is true in the way
that a locked door is secure: technically accurate, and not much help to the
person standing outside it.

Here is what actually happens on Veritix, in order, with no steps skipped.

## The money never sits with us

When a buyer pays for a ticket, the funds do not move into a Veritix balance and
they do not sit in a platform bank account. They move into an **escrow account**
on the Stellar network — a Stellar account whose transfers are public and
verifiable by anyone with a browser.

This is the single decision that shapes everything else, so it is worth being
blunt about what it buys and what it costs.

What it buys: the organizer cannot pay out early, buyers cannot be charged twice
for the same seat, and neither side has to take the other's word for what
happened. The ledger is the record. It is not our record.

What it costs: escrow accounts are public. The addresses, the amounts, and the
transaction identifiers are visible to anyone who looks. That is a real
constraint, and the reason the Privacy Policy says plainly that we do not put
personal data on-chain and that the wallet addresses we do are permanently
public.

## Revenue splits are decided before the first sale

When an organizer publishes an event, they define the split: this percentage to
me, this percentage to the artist, this percentage to the venue. It is fixed at
that moment and it cannot be edited afterwards.

That restriction is the whole point. A split you can change after buyers have
paid is not a promise, it is an intention, and every buyer who agreed to it is
holding a document that has since been revised. So the split is locked when the
event is published, and the version the buyers agreed to is the version that
settles.

## Settlement is one transaction, not a batch job

When the event completes and the settlement window closes, the escrow releases
the shares in a single transaction. The organizer, the artist, and the venue are
paid in the same operation, and the fee we deduct is deducted from the escrow
account before the release rather than invoiced afterwards.

There is no payout to request. There is no invoice to chase. The transfer either
appears in the ledger or it does not, and the report in the organizer dashboard
reads from the ledger rather than from an internal table that might disagree with
it.

## Refunds, before and after settlement

Before settlement, while the escrow is open, a refund is straightforward: the
funds are still in the escrow account, and a refund is a transfer back to the
original payment method. If an organizer cancels, every ticket sold is refundable
that way — whether or not individual buyers ask.

After settlement, a refund is different, and pretending otherwise is the most
common misleading thing said about on-chain ticketing. Once the release has
happened, the funds are in the recipients' accounts. A refund is a **new
transfer out of the organizer's settled share**. It shows up in their report as a
refund rather than as a reduction in ticket revenue, because that is what it is.

This is a real trade-off, not a bug. It means we cannot hold money back after
settlement to make a post-settlement refund painless, and we could not if we
wanted to — the release is unconditional once its conditions are met. What it
means in practice is that the protection buyers get is strongest before the event
and weaker after it, and an organizer who plans to cancel has to behave
accordingly. The Terms say this. It seemed better to say it than to bury it.

## Why this is worth the complexity

We could have done a much simpler thing: hold the money ourselves, mark orders
paid when a webhook fires, and pay organizers weekly. That is what most
platforms do and it works fine, right up until the moment somebody disputes it —
at which point the answer is whatever our database says, and the buyer has no way
to check.

The on-chain version is slower to build, harder to operate, and more expensive
per ticket. What it buys is that the most important question a ticketing
platform gets asked — *did the money actually move, and to whom* — has an answer
that does not depend on trusting us. That is the bet.
